Stories

It might seem redundant to have a section for stories when fragments of them are already scattered across this page, hidden in plain sight. These are not narratives with a beginning, middle, and end. They are something else entirely. There are times, when a feeling, be it incredible happiness or a crushing sadness, becomes too immense to contain, that I must give it a shape. I use an app as a digital journal, a vault for events that vary wildly in their nature. What is interesting is not what I wrote, but that I stopped. Over the years, the tendency to document these moments faded, even though the moments themselves did not. Reading them now is like reading the words of a stranger, or perhaps a madman. I have pushed these memories so far into the recesses of my mind that I can no longer access the person who lived them. This distance is likely good for my mental health, yet it is profoundly sad to acknowledge the ease with which I can forget myself. You will likely never read one of those stories here. The way they are described, the raw and unfiltered intensity of the feeling, is too explicit, too descriptive, too personal. They are not maps; they are the territory itself, jagged and breathing. Some things must not be shared. So why include this section at all? First, to remember that they exist. They are proof of a life lived intensely. Second, to forget that they exist. By confining them to this abstract acknowledgement, I hope to release their hold on me. It is a silly, stupid contradiction. The digital vault could be deleted with a click, but I am not ready for that final silence. Perhaps this section, then, is a compromise. A new altar built beside the old well, in the hope that if the need to write ever returns, I will bring it here, instead of there.



Luca Must Participate

I have always collected people. Not as a hobby, but as a quiet compulsion. I am a student of their geometries, the way they orbit each other, creating constellations of meaning. And then I found a binary star: Luca and Fuca.

They had a gravity born of a shared childhood. He was a nova of laughter, all goofy light and heat. She was a quieter, denser body, her intelligence a steady, pulling force. To be near them was to be caught in a stable, comforting system. For a time, I was a happy satellite, tracing their patterns.

Our primary ritual was chess. We played all its variations, but our favorite was drunk chess, where strategy bled into confession. Yet, our universe had a strange, unshakable law. It was always me against Luca, or Luca against Fuca, or me and Luca against Fuca. The one pairing that never manifested was me against Fuca. It was the silent, negative space in our constellation, a void we all acknowledged but never gave a name. It was like noticing a shared, faint scar on each other's hands and knowing, without a word, that you must never ask how it was made.

The understanding was this: I was a little better than Luca. Fuca was better than both of us. So, to maintain the balance, the equilibrium that made our system function, Luca had to participate. It was the fundamental physics of our friendship. I accepted this law. We all did. For a long, serene while, none of us questioned the universe we inhabited.

But all orbits decay. The day came when we were to be pulled in different directions by the tides of life. On our last evening, we fell into our old ritual. Jenga, cards, the easy comfort of familiar games, until the drinks gave us courage for the main event: drunk chess.

And then Luca, the only one of us whose gravity was weak enough to speak the unspeakable, asked the question. "Why don't you two ever play each other?"

The air in the room changed. It became thin, charged. We were all competitive, but Luca's goofy light usually diffused the tension. Now, that light was gone. I was terrified. Somewhere in the deep, silent parts of me, I had always wanted this, but to want a thing is to risk its loss. I went to wash my face, and in the sterile bathroom light, I planned my opening. This was my chance to finally map Fuca's territory alone.

When I returned, the board was set. Luca had poured the water, opened the beers. The ceremony was complete. Outside, a drizzle began, and people outside quickened their steps into blurs. I took my seat. Fuca’s face was a placid lake, but her eyes were the sharp, dark stones at the bottom. She gave me the white pieces.

And then I knew. I had already lost.

It is a certain kind of knowing, the kind you feel when you step onto a path and realize the destination was decided long before you took the first step. Her moves were not just moves; they were revelations. e4, c6, d4, d5. The Caro-Kann. My defense. She was a French and Dutch player; this was a message. She was not just playing to win; she was playing me.

The game unfolded like a predestined tragedy. My solid position was an illusion. I blundered, and she was a perfect predator, seizing the advantage without mercy. She traded down, simplifying the board until her victory was a mathematical certainty. She was a rook ahead. I was drowning in the silence, sweat beading on my skin like condensation on a cold glass. The air was thick enough to drink. She was tense, too, which made no sense. She had won. But that is chess; it doesn't just test your mind, it suffocates your soul.

Then, she blundered.

It was not a mistake. It was an impossibility. A star forgetting how to burn. Luca gasped. I looked at her, and she was only looking at the board, her expression unreadable. And then she did it again. Another catastrophic, beautiful error. The material was equal.

My resilience had nothing to do with it. Her game had been flawless. There was no world in which she missed those moves. Unless.

As I stared at the board, I saw it. The ghost in the machine. The clumsy, hopeful, ridiculous sequence of moves that could only belong to one person. It was Luca’s strategy. His signature was all over her blunders.

And in that moment, I saw the truth of our entire friendship. It was not about chess. It was not about winning. The irritation, the tension, the unbearable weight of competition—it was a black hole that threatened to collapse our beautiful, three-body system. And Fuca, who was always better than me at everything important, had understood this long before I did. She was sacrificing her victory not to let me win, but to save us. To preserve the one thing that made any of it matter.

So, I made my move. I blundered, too. Not toward defeat, but toward understanding. A silent message sent across the checkered battlefield: I see you. I see what you are protecting.

There is a force that binds certain atoms together. It is not love, nor is it friendship, in any simple sense. It is a necessary, third element that prevents a volatile reaction. A catalyst for peace. A source of light.

That is when I understood, with a clarity that has never left me, that Luca must participate.
